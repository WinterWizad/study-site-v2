## 2.1 Multi-sided platforms and network effects

> **A multi-sided platform (MSP) is an intermediary that enables direct interactions between two or more distinct groups of participants — "sides" — and captures value by solving the coordination problem that would exist without it.**

The definition has three testable parts:

| Part | What it means | Exam signal |
|---|---|---|
| **distinct groups of participants** | sides have different needs: buyers vs sellers, riders vs drivers, advertisers vs users, developers vs users | A4 asks for *types* — name the sides |
| **enables direct interactions** | the platform does not buy and resell; it matches and facilitates | distinguishes MSP from a reseller/wholesaler |
| **captures value** | fees, commissions, subscriptions, data, advertising — the business model | Unit 3's business models |

### Network effects — the engine of platform value

> **A network effect exists when the value of a product or service to one user increases as the number of other users increases.** It is the *positive feedback loop* that makes platforms winner-take-most.

| Type | Direction | Example | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Direct (same-side)** | more users → more value for *same-side* users | telephone, WhatsApp, Facebook (more friends → more value) | Metcalfe's law: value ∝ n² |
| **Indirect (cross-side)** | more users on side A → more value for side B | Uber (more drivers → shorter wait for riders; more riders → more income for drivers), credit cards (more merchants → more value for cardholders; more cardholders → more value for merchants) | the platform's core coordination function |
| **Negative (congestion)** | more users → *less* value | crowded marketplace search, bandwidth saturation, review spam | platforms design against this (matching algorithms, curation) |

**Positive feedback** is the dynamic: network effects → more users → stronger effects → even more users. The loop stops at *market saturation*, *multi-homing* (users on multiple platforms), or *competition*.

### Why network effects matter in digital markets — B13 (5 marks ≈ 200 words)

> **Shape: define → direct vs indirect → why they matter (three reasons) → the lock-in consequence. ~200 words.**

Network effects are the **structural reason digital markets tend to concentration**. Three reasons they matter:

1. **They create increasing returns to scale.** In a traditional factory, the thousandth unit costs the same as the first (constant returns). In a platform, the thousandth user *adds* value to all existing users — the marginal value of a user is positive. This is why platforms invest heavily in user acquisition before monetisation.
2. **They generate switching costs endogenously.** The more you use a platform (data, connections, reputation, learned workflows), the more you lose by leaving. The platform does not need a contract to lock you in; the network does it.
3. **They shape competition: winner-take-most, not perfect competition.** Two platforms with similar features do not split the market 50/50; the one with the larger network pulls ahead, and the gap widens until the smaller exits or finds a defensible niche. This is why "digital monopolization" (A3) is a *tendency*, not an accident.

**The lock-in consequence.** Network effects + switching costs + multi-homing costs = the moat. A platform that achieves critical mass on both sides becomes extremely hard to displace. The exam answer for B13 must name the three reasons and the lock-in link.

## 2.2 Lock-in and switching costs

> **Switching cost is the economic, psychological or contractual cost a user incurs when moving from one platform (or product) to another.** In digital markets, switching costs are the *retention mechanism* that turns network effects into durable market power.

### Sources of switching costs — the list A5 expects (2 marks ≈ 55 words)

> **Four main sources:** (1) **Data and history portability** — user-generated content, transaction history, ratings, credit scores, trained models that cannot be exported or are costly to recreate. (2) **Network and relationship specificity** — the connections, followers, supplier relationships, buyer reputation that exist only on that platform. (3) **Learning and workflow investment** — time spent mastering the interface, APIs, integrations, customisations. (4) **Contractual and financial lock-in** — subscriptions, early-termination penalties, hardware bundles, proprietary standards. Together they make the *effective* cost of switching higher than the *nominal* price difference.

*(59 words. Four named sources, one clause each.)*

## 2.3 Digital monopolization — A3 (2 marks ≈ 55 words)

> **Digital monopolization is the tendency for digital markets to concentrate around one or a few dominant platforms, driven by the interaction of network effects, near-zero marginal costs, data-driven increasing returns, and endogenous switching costs.** It is not a legal monopoly — entry is technically possible — but the *effective* barrier is the installed base: a new entrant must overcome the incumbent's network effects *and* the users' switching costs simultaneously. The result is winner-take-most dynamics in platform markets, not the competitive fragmentation of traditional goods markets.

*(58 words. Mechanism + why it's not a legal monopoly + result.)*

## 2.4 Types of multi-sided platforms — A4 (2 marks ≈ 55 words)

> **Four canonical types, named by the sides they connect:**
> 1. **Marketplace / transaction platforms** — buyers ↔ sellers (Daraz, Amazon, eBay, Airbnb).
> 2. **Payment platforms** — consumers ↔ merchants ↔ banks (eSewa, Khalti, Visa, Mastercard, Stripe).
> 3. **Social / content platforms** — users ↔ users ↔ advertisers (Facebook, YouTube, TikTok, LinkedIn).
> 4. **Innovation / developer platforms** — developers ↔ users (iOS/Android, Windows, AWS/Azure/GCP, Shopify).
>
> *(Other acceptable answers: ride-hailing — riders ↔ drivers; food delivery — diners ↔ restaurants ↔ couriers; dating — seekers ↔ seekers. The four above are the textbook categories.)*

*(58 words. Four types, sides named, one example each.)*

## 2.5 Digital Adoption Index and OECD Digital Government Index

The syllabus names two indices. The **Digital Adoption Index (DAI)** is a World Bank measure (0–1) covering three pillars: **people** (internet users, mobile subscriptions, digital skills), **government** (online services, digital ID, open data), and **business** (website, e-commerce, cloud, digital payments). Nepal's DAI is low (~0.35), reflecting the reach gap the Agro-tech Concern case illustrates.

The **OECD Digital Government Index (DGI)** is the one B12 asks for. It has **six dimensions**, each scored 0–1:

| Dimension | What it measures | Nepal context (illustrative) |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Digital by design** | government processes built digital-first, not digitised after | Nagarik app (digital ID) — yes; most ministry workflows — still paper-first |
| **2. Data-driven public sector** | data used for policy, service design, performance management | limited — data silos across ministries, no whole-of-government analytics |
| **3. Government as a platform** | shared digital infrastructure (identity, payments, data exchange) exposed as APIs | connectIPS (payments), X-Road pilot (data exchange) — early stage |
| **4. Open by default** | open data, open source, open standards, transparency | Open Data Portal exists; dataset freshness and granularity are low |
| **5. User-driven** | services designed around user needs, co-creation, feedback loops | Nagarik and Mero Kitta have feedback; most portals do not |
| **6. Proactiveness** | anticipating needs, predictive services, life-event bundling | absent — services are reactive, application-based |

### B12 · "Describe the dimensions of the OECD Digital Government index." [5]

> **Shape: name the index → list all six dimensions with one-line descriptions → one Nepal illustration. ~200 words.**

The **OECD Digital Government Index (DGI)** benchmarks governments' digital maturity across **six dimensions**:

1. **Digital by design** — processes built digital-first, not digitised after the fact. *Nepal: Nagarik digital ID is digital-by-design; most ministry workflows remain paper-first.*
2. **Data-driven public sector** — data used for policy, service design and performance management. *Nepal: data silos across ministries; no whole-of-government analytics platform.*
3. **Government as a platform** — shared digital infrastructure (identity, payments, data exchange) exposed as reusable APIs. *Nepal: connectIPS for payments, X-Road pilot for data exchange — early stage.*
4. **Open by default** — open data, open source, open standards, transparency. *Nepal: Open Data Portal exists; dataset freshness and granularity are low.*
5. **User-driven** — services designed around user needs, co-creation, feedback loops. *Nepal: Nagarik and Mero Kitta have feedback mechanisms; most portals do not.*
6. **Proactiveness** — anticipating needs, predictive services, life-event bundling. *Nepal: absent — services are reactive and application-based.*

The DGI matters because it measures *capability*, not just presence — a government can have a portal (digital by design) but still be reactive, siloed and closed. Nepal scores low on dimensions 2, 4 and 6, which is exactly the governance gap the Agro-tech Concern case (Unit 6) exposes.

*(212 words)*

> A network effect exists when the value of a product or service to one user increases as the number of other users increases. **Direct (same-side)** effects: more users → more value for same-side users (WhatsApp, Metcalfe's law). **Indirect (cross-side)** effects: more users on side A → more value for side B (Uber: drivers ↔ riders; credit cards: merchants ↔ cardholders).

## 2.7 Traps

| Trap | Why it costs marks | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Defining network effects only as "more users = better" | misses direct vs indirect distinction; B13 expects both | name direct (same-side) and indirect (cross-side) with examples |
| Listing "marketplace, ride-hailing, food delivery, dating" as four types for A4 | these are all *marketplace variants* — same category | use the four canonical categories: marketplace, payment, social/content, innovation/developer |
| Saying switching costs are only "contractual" | misses the endogenous, network-driven sources A5/B13 test | four sources: data/history, network/relationships, learning/workflow, contractual |
| Writing "digital monopolization = monopoly" | it's a tendency, not a legal status; the distinction is the mark | "tendency to concentrate... not a legal monopoly... effective barrier is installed base" |
| Forgetting the OECD DGI has six dimensions | B12 says "dimensions" (plural); missing one loses marks | memorise all six: digital by design, data-driven, platform, open, user-driven, proactive |
| Giving a generic Nepal example for DGI | B12 expects the dimensions, not a story | one illustration per dimension, tied to the dimension's definition |
| Confusing Digital Adoption Index (World Bank, 3 pillars) with OECD DGI (6 dimensions) | they are different indices; the syllabus names both | DAI = people/government/business; DGI = six dimensions above |
