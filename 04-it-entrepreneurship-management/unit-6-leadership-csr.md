## 6.1 Leadership styles in entrepreneurship (A9, C19)

### A9 · "Define leadership style." [2] 🎯 2-mark

> Leadership style is the consistent pattern of behaviour a leader uses to influence, guide, and direct a team — encompassing decision-making, communication, delegation, and motivation. It ranges from **autocratic** (leader decides) to **democratic** (team input and shared decisions) to **laissez-faire** (team self-directs), with **transformational** (inspire change) and **servant** (serve the team) as modern variants.

*(52 words. Definition + spectrum + five named styles.)*

### C19 · "Explain different leadership styles used in startups." [10] 🎯 10-mark

> **Shape: 4–5 styles, each with definition + when it works + startup context. ~400 words.**

**Five leadership styles for startups:**

1. **Autocratic** — leader decides unilaterally; top-down commands. *When it works:* crisis, early-stage chaos, regulatory compliance, time-critical decisions. *Startup context:* founder sets non-negotiables (safety standards, legal compliance, cash discipline) in Year 1.

2. **Democratic (Participative)** — leader involves team in decisions; consensus-seeking. *When it works:* complex problems needing diverse input, building buy-in, culture formation. *Startup context:* product roadmap prioritisation, hiring decisions, culture values definition.

3. **Transformational** — leader inspires with vision, challenges assumptions, develops people. *When it works:* scaling phase, culture change, attracting talent, pivot moments. *Startup context:* articulating "why we exist" (e.g., "safe mobility for all"), rallying team through funding winter.

4. **Servant Leadership** — leader serves the team; removes obstacles, empowers, grows people. *When it works:* knowledge-worker teams, high autonomy, strong culture. *Startup context:* engineering/product teams; leader clears blockers, advocates for resources, celebrates team wins.

5. **Situational / Adaptive** — leader flexes style based on team maturity, task, and urgency (Hersey-Blanchard). *When it works:* across stages, when the style matches team maturity, task, and urgency. *Startup context:* directive for safety/cash; participative for product; transformational for vision; servant for team growth.

**The startup reality:** no single style works forever. Founders may start directive, involve the team as culture forms, communicate a vision while scaling, and practise servant leadership daily. Situational leaders diagnose the moment and choose.

*(404 words)*

### Board B6 · "How can ICT be aligned with leadership characteristics?" [5] 🎯 5-mark

> ICT should support the leadership trait that matters for the current stage. **Opportunity recognition** improves when analytics expose changing customer behaviour and market signals. **Resilience** improves when teams retain experiment, customer-feedback, and incident data, then learn from failure without blame. **Resource orchestration** improves through shared dashboards, cloud tools, and communication platforms that connect specialists. **Vision and execution** improve when goals, priorities, owners, and deadlines remain visible in project and performance tools. **Servant leadership** improves when ICT removes routine work, supports asynchronous participation, and gives employees easier access to information and feedback. ICT can also weaken leadership if a founder becomes controlling, uses surveillance instead of trust, or creates a tool-heavy culture. The fit is therefore deliberate: choose software that improves the behaviour the venture needs, train leaders to interpret its outputs, and keep final decisions human and accountable.
>
> **Example:** A ride-sharing startup needs faster incident response. It uses a shared dashboard to flag repeated ride cancellations, live incident chat to connect the operations lead with the driver and rider, and an incident log to track the cause and corrective action. The dashboard shows patterns, but the team reviews context before acting. Access to dashboards must match responsibility, and personal data should remain limited to what the team needs. Algorithms may support decisions, but humans remain accountable. Regular team conversations remain necessary because technology cannot reveal fear, conflict, or lack of skill.

*(231 words)*

## 6.2 Building effective teams

| Principle | What it means | Startup application |
|---|---|---|
| **Hire for capability and learning** | balance role skills, expected behaviours, and learning potential; do not hire on "culture fit" alone | define 3–4 core values; interview for them |

| **Psychological safety** | speak up without fear; fail fast, learn fast | blameless post-mortems; leader admits mistakes first |

## 6.3 Conflict resolution techniques (B17)

> **Conflict in startups is inevitable — co-founder disputes, priority clashes, resource allocation, equity splits. The skill is resolving it fast without relationship damage.**

### B17 · "Explain the conflict resolution techniques within the team." [5] 🎯 5-mark

> **Shape: 5 techniques, one-line each + when to use. ~200 words.**

Five conflict resolution techniques for startup teams:

1. **Collaborating (Win-Win)** — both parties work together to find a solution meeting both needs. *Use when:* relationship matters, issue is complex, time allows. *Example:* co-founders disagree on product direction → workshop to integrate both visions.

2. **Compromising (Split the Difference)** — each gives up something for a middle ground. *Use when:* time pressure, equal power, temporary fix. *Example:* marketing vs product on launch date → launch MVP now, full features in v1.1.

3. **Accommodating (Yield)** — one yields to preserve relationship. *Use when:* issue matters more to other party, relationship > outcome. *Example:* designer insists on UI detail; PM yields to build trust.

4. **Competing (Assert)** — one pushes own view, leverages authority. *Use when:* emergency, safety, ethics, non-negotiable. *Example:* founder overrides team on safety feature before launch.

5. **Avoiding (Defer)** — postpone or sidestep. *Use when:* trivial issue, emotions high, more info needed. *Example:* heated debate on office snacks → "let's decide next week."

**The startup rule:** choose by issue. Collaborate when both interests can be integrated; compete only for firm boundaries; compromise when a timely workable outcome matters; avoid only when delay is useful.

*(208 words)*

## 6.4 Motivating employees in startups (C20a)

> **C20 is a split question: "How do you motivate employees in startups? Explain the digital marketing and sales techniques." Answer in two headed halves.**

### C20a · "How do you motivate employees in startups?" [5] 🎯 5-mark

> **Shape: 4 drivers of motivation + startup context + Nepal example. ~200 words.**

Motivation in startups comes from four drivers, not just salary:

1. **Purpose & Vision** — people work harder when they believe in the "why". *Action:* articulate a compelling mission ("safe mobility for all"); connect daily work to customer impact. *Nepal application:* explain how the venture improves transport safety, access, or driver earnings.

2. **Autonomy & Ownership** — give people control over *how* they achieve outcomes. *Action:* set clear outcomes (OKRs), let teams choose methods; avoid micromanagement. *Nepal example:* engineering team chooses tech stack within architecture guardrails.

3. **Mastery & Growth** — people want to get better at what they do. *Action:* learning budget, mentorship, stretch projects, visible career path. *Nepal example:* quarterly "learning Friday"; sponsor AWS/Azure certs; internal tech talks.

4. **Recognition & Fair Rewards** — timely, specific recognition; equity or profit-share aligned with contribution. *Action:* public shoutouts, spot bonuses, transparent vesting, and performance-linked rewards. *Nepal application:* recognise safe service and customer support, but pay a fair market salary and follow employment law even when cash is tight.

**The startup reality:** cash may be limited, but purpose, autonomy, mastery, and recognition add value. They do not replace fair pay or lawful employment terms.

*(204 words)*

## 6.5 Networking skills for entrepreneurs

| Skill | What it means | Startup application |
|---|---|---|
| **Strategic mapping** | identify who you need (investors, mentors, partners, talent) | maintain a "top 50" list; review monthly |
| **Value-first approach** | give before you ask; share insights, intros, resources | "How can I help?" before "Can you invest?" |
| **Warm introductions** | use a mutual connection to lower first-contact risk | ask a portfolio founder for an investor introduction |
| **Personal brand** | consistent voice on LinkedIn/Twitter; share lessons, not wins | "What I learned raising our seed round" |
| **Follow-up discipline** | 24-hour thank you; monthly check-in; CRM for network | HubSpot/Airtable for "top 50" contacts |

### A10 · "What do you mean by corporate social responsibility (CSR)?" [2] 🎯 2-mark

> CSR is a company's responsibility to operate ethically and contribute to positive social, environmental, and stakeholder outcomes while pursuing profit. It can include voluntary actions, but statutory duties may also apply depending on the company and jurisdiction. For startups, responsibility should shape products, employment, sourcing, data use, and community impact from the start.

## 6.8 Ethical marketing strategies (C21a)

> **C21 is a split question: "Discuss about ethical marketing strategies. What are the essential elements of a business plan?" Answer in two headed halves.**

### C21a · "Discuss about ethical marketing strategies." [5] 🎯 5-mark

> **Shape: 4 principles + Nepal ride-share example. ~200 words.**

Ethical marketing means promoting truthfully, respecting user autonomy, and avoiding manipulation — building trust as a long-term asset.

**Four principles:**

1. **Truthfulness & Transparency** — no false claims, hidden fees, or bait-and-switch. *Startup:* upfront fare, no surge surprise, clear cancellation policy.
2. **Respect for Privacy** — consent-based data use; no dark patterns; easy opt-out. *Startup:* rider data used only for ride matching; no sale to third parties; clear privacy policy.
3. **Inclusivity & Non-Discrimination** — marketing reflects diverse users; no stereotypes. *Startup:* women riders featured; accessibility features highlighted; language options (Nepali/English).
4. **Social Responsibility** — marketing supports positive behaviour; no exploitation of vulnerability. *Startup:* promote helmet use, safe riding, driver welfare; no "drink and ride" campaigns.

**The business case:** ethical marketing builds brand trust and can strengthen a durable moat. Unethical marketing may win short-term, but loses long-term.

*(204 words)*

## 6.9 Case study: Tata Group's CSR and its impact on society

> **Scope:** This analysis uses Tata Group's public governance and sustainability reporting. It does not claim that CSR alone produced each social outcome. ⚠ unverified: the cited reports are not saved locally, so this note uses no percentage figures.

| Stage | Tata Group example | How it creates societal impact | What to judge |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Governance and ownership** | Tata Trusts hold a controlling share of Tata Sons. The group uses board-level CSR oversight and formal impact review | Long-term ownership gives philanthropy a stable institutional base. Board oversight connects social spending with company governance | Are funds protected from short-term pressure? Does the board measure outcomes and publish results? |
| **Financial commitment** | The group follows a rule-based CSR commitment. India's Companies Act, 2013 requires qualifying companies to spend a minimum of average net profit on CSR | A rule-based minimum makes CSR part of operating finance rather than optional year-end charity | Spending is an input, not an outcome. The question is what changed for beneficiaries |
| **Capability through businesses** | Tata Steel has worked on education, health, water, and livelihoods in communities near its operations. Tata Power has supported rural electrification and renewable-energy access | The company uses its operating knowledge, employees, and partnerships to address problems that public agencies alone cannot solve | Do the programmes reach excluded or marginalised groups? Can the business continue the service after funding ends? |
| **Education and skills** | Tata Trusts and Tata companies support education, literacy, and STEM learning, including programmes for girls and disadvantaged learners | Better skills expand employment choices and economic participation. STEM programmes can also increase the future supply of technical workers | Measure learning gains, completion, employment, and wage outcomes rather than participant counts alone |
| **Environment and rural livelihoods** | Tata Power's renewable work and Tata Steel's community programmes connect environmental action with local economic participation | Renewable electricity improves access and reduces environmental harm. Livelihood programmes link cleaner operations with household income | Projects must include local participation and credible baseline data. Avoid counting planned benefits as achieved ones |

**Impact chain:** **Input** — CSR funds and employee expertise → **Output** — schools, clinics, water systems, training, or energy access → **Outcome** — improved learning, health, skills, income, or reliable energy → **Long-term impact** — greater local capability and inclusion. Tata's approach is strong when it moves through this chain and publishes comparable evidence.

**Limit:** CSR cannot replace public services or good core business. A company should select issues connected to its operating knowledge, partner with credible local institutions, avoid replacing public funding, and report failures as well as successes.

**Exam conclusion:** Tata's case shows CSR as governance, not charity. The ownership structure, legal minimum, board oversight, business capability, and impact measurement make the model durable. A Nepali startup should copy the **process** — choose a material issue, set measurable outcomes, assign budget and ownership, evaluate results, and publish the learning — rather than copy large-company spending.
