## 2.1 Techniques for creative thinking (A3)

> **Creative thinking techniques are structured methods to generate novel ideas — they move you from "waiting for inspiration" to "producing options on demand."**

### Four examinable techniques (pick any two for A3)

| Technique | How it works | When to use |
|---|---|---|
| **Brainstorming** | group idea generation; defer judgement (do not criticise immediately); aim for many ideas, then build on them | early divergence (generating many different options), team setting |
| **SCAMPER** | Substitute, Combine, Adapt, Modify, Put to another use, Eliminate, Reverse — apply to existing product/service | improving or pivoting an existing idea |
| **Mind Mapping** | central problem → branches (who, what, where, how, why) → sub-branches | solo exploration, visual thinkers |
| **Trend Analysis** | scan tech/market/social trends → ask "what if this continues?" / "what is the opposite?" | spotting new opportunities (links to B14) |

### A3 · "List any two techniques for creative thinking." [2] 🎯 2-mark

> Two techniques: (1) **Brainstorming** — group session with deferred judgement, aim for quantity first, then build on others' ideas; best for early divergence. (2) **SCAMPER** — apply seven prompts (Substitute, Combine, Adapt, Modify, Put to another use, Eliminate, Reverse) to an existing product or service to generate variants; best for improving or pivoting.

*(54 words. Two names + one-line how-it-works + when-to-use each.)*

### Board A2 · "What is the concept of creative thinking?" [2] 🎯 2-mark

> Creative thinking is the deliberate use of original, varied, and useful ideas to solve a problem or create value. It changes how a founder frames the problem, generates options, and tests possible solutions. Techniques include brainstorming, mind mapping, SCAMPER, and design thinking. In entrepreneurship, it prevents founders from accepting the first obvious solution.

*(53 words)*

## 2.2 Market needs identification (B12)

> **Market needs identification is the systematic process of discovering what target users truly need — not what they say they want — and validating that the need is urgent, pervasive, and worth paying for.**

### The process (four steps for B12)

| Step | What you do | Tools |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Observe** | watch users in context; note workarounds, complaints, "hacks" | contextual inquiry, shadowing, forum/social listening |
| **2. Interview** | open-ended questions about problems, not solutions | "Tell me about the last time you..." / "What is the hardest part of...?" |
| **3. Quantify** | estimate how many have the need, how often, how much they'd pay | surveys, keyword search volume, market reports |
| **4. Validate** | build a minimum test (landing page, demo, concierge MVP) | smoke test, pre-sell, letter of intent |

### B12 · "How do you identify market needs for a new IT idea?" [5] 🎯 5-mark

> **Shape: four-step process + one Nepal example per step. ~200 words.**

Identifying market needs follows four steps:

1. **Observe** — watch users in context; note workarounds, complaints, "hacks". *Nepal example:* observe Kathmandu commuters queuing for microbuses, using multiple apps, and asking drivers for cash discounts.
2. **Interview** — open-ended questions about problems, not solutions ("Tell me about the last time you..." / "What is the hardest part of...?"). *Nepal example:* interview a small sample of riders about peak-hour pain points — safety, cancellation, and fare uncertainty.
3. **Quantify** — estimate how many have the need, how often it occurs, and what they already pay. *Nepal example:* count affected commuters in a defined route or workplace area, then test price sensitivity through a survey and pilot booking.
4. **Validate** — build a minimum test (landing page, demo, concierge MVP). *Nepal example:* launch a WhatsApp-based ride-booking pilot in one corridor; measure completion, repeat use, complaints, and willingness to pay before building the full app.

*(208 words)*

## 2.3 Target market definition (A4)

> **A target market is the specific segment of users you design for, message to, and sell to first — defined by demographics, geography, behaviour, and need.**

### A4 · "What do you mean by target market?" [2] 🎯 2-mark

> A target market is the specific user segment you design for, message to, and sell to first — defined by demographics (age, income, occupation), geography (Kathmandu Valley, Tier-2 cities), behaviour (frequency, channel preference), and need (the problem you solve). Example: for a ride-sharing app, "Kathmandu professionals aged 25–40 who commute daily, value safety over price, and pay digitally."

## 2.4 Evaluating business ideas

> **Not every idea is worth pursuing. Use a filter to decide.**

### Quick evaluation framework (three filters)

| Filter | Question | Pass = |
|---|---|---|
| **Desirability** | Do users *need* this urgently? | validated need (B12 step 4) |
| **Feasibility** | Can we build/deliver it with our resources? | tech stack, team, capital, timeline |
| **Viability** | Can it make money sustainably? | unit economics (LTV > CAC), path to scale |

**For the exam:** if asked to "evaluate an idea," name the three filters and apply them in one sentence each.

## 2.5 Prototyping development

**Prototype terms in plain English:** a **wireframe** is a simple screen sketch; **fidelity** means how realistic the prototype is; an **MVP (minimum viable product)** is the smallest testable version; and **usability** means how easily a user can complete the task.

| Prototype type | Fidelity | Purpose | Nepal example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Paper / wireframe** | low | test screen flow and rough ideas | sketch the ride-booking flow on paper |
| **Clickable mockup** | medium | test ease of use and get feedback | Figma prototype for a driver app |
| **Concierge MVP** | people do the work behind the service | test demand before writing the full app | WhatsApp group for manual ride matching |
| **Functional MVP** | the core feature works | test repeat use and whether each sale earns money | basic app with booking and payment only |

**Key principle:** start with the lowest fidelity that answers your riskiest assumption.

### Board B2 · "Explain the steps of prototyping development." [5] 🎯 5-mark

**Plain-language note:** a **wizard-of-oz** prototype looks automated to the customer, but people secretly do the work behind the screen.

> Prototyping turns an idea into something users can react to. **1. Identify the riskiest assumption.** Decide whether the main uncertainty is demand, usability, price, or technical feasibility. **2. Define the core experience.** State the user's job and the minimum flow needed to complete it. **3. Choose the lowest-fidelity prototype.** Use paper sketches or a clickable wireframe for flows; use a concierge service or wizard-of-oz delivery when behaviour matters more than technology; build a functional MVP when integration must be tested. **4. Build quickly.** Include only the risky core and record assumptions behind each choice. **5. Test with real users.** Observe users completing the task, collect confusion and failure points, and avoid explaining the prototype. **6. Measure and revise.** Track completion, errors, time, repeat use, and willingness to pay. **7. Decide.** Continue, change, or abandon the assumption before scaling. The prototype is an experiment, not a miniature finished product.
>
> Keep the prototype separate from the final product. Document what was learned, not just what was built. A useful test has a hypothesis, a task, and a success measure. For example, if trust is uncertain, test a concierge booking before building driver matching. If navigation is uncertain, test the route map before optimising payment. This prevents a polished prototype from hiding a weak business idea.

*(213 words)*

## 2.6 Current technology trend analysis (B14)

> **Trend analysis in idea generation means scanning technology, market, and social trends — then asking "what new problems or solutions does this create?"**

### B14 · "Discuss the roles of trend analysis in idea generation." [5] 🎯 5-mark

**Plain-language note:** **AI** means artificial intelligence, **ML** means machine learning, **B2C** means business-to-customer, and **B2B** means business-to-business.

> **Shape: three roles + Nepal example each. ~200 words.**

Trend analysis plays three roles in idea generation:

1. **Opportunity spotting** — a rising trend creates new needs. *Example:* AI/ML trend → Nepali startups building document automation for banks (KYC, loan processing) and government (licence processing).
2. **Timing validation** — entering too early = no infrastructure; too late = saturated. *Example:* ride-sharing grew as smartphone use, app-based services, and digital payments became familiar. A new entrant should test whether local payment, driver trust, and connectivity are ready before scaling.
3. **Differentiation** — trend + contrarian angle = unique positioning. *Example:* while others built B2C food delivery, a startup built B2B cloud kitchen management (SaaS for restaurants) — same trend (digital food), different slice.

*(204 words)*

## 2.7 Competitor analysis (B13)

> **Competitor analysis is the systematic mapping of who else solves the same problem — their strengths, weaknesses, positioning, and your defensible wedge.**

### B13 · "Write a short note on competitor analysis." [5] 🎯 5-mark

> **Shape: why it matters → framework (4 quadrants) → Nepal example. ~200 words.**

Competitor analysis maps who else solves your user's problem so you find a **defensible wedge** — a small advantage that competitors cannot copy quickly. Use a **four-quadrant framework**:

| Quadrant | What to map |
|---|---|
| **Direct competitors** | same problem, same solution (Pathao vs Tootle vs inDrive) |
| **Indirect competitors** | same problem, different solution (microbuses, taxis, own bike) |
| **Substitutes** | different problem, same budget (walking, company shuttle) |
| **Potential entrants** | adjacent players who could pivot (food delivery apps adding rides) |

**For each competitor, track:** pricing, unique selling points (USPs), user reviews (Play Store/App Store), funding, partnerships, and technology stack (the tools used to build the service).

*Nepal example:* Pathao (early mover and later super-app), Tootle (began with a Facebook-group MVP and focused on motorcycle rides), inDrive (price-negotiation model with global backing). A candidate wedge for a new entrant is **safety-first mobility** with verified drivers, SOS, and ride tracking, but the team must validate the gap with riders and drivers before claiming it is underserved.

*(208 words)*

## 2.8 Consumer behaviour insight

| Insight | What it means | Nepal application |
|---|---|---|
| **Price sensitivity** | high for students, low for professionals | tiered pricing: economy/business |
| **Trust signals** | ratings, verification, brand, word-of-mouth | driver photos, SOS, insurance badge |
| **Convenience > features** | one-tap booking beats feature-rich complex UI | simple flow: open → book → ride |
| **Network effects** | more riders → more drivers → shorter wait | referral bonuses, launch density |
| **Payment preference** | cash still dominant; digital growing | wallet + cash option mandatory; NRB 2023 recorded cash at about 78% of transaction value |

### Board C1 · "How different ICT tools can be used for effective consumer insight? Explain with example." [10] 🎯 10-mark

**Plain-language note:** **market mining** means searching online data for unmet needs; **social listening** means reading public posts, reviews, and complaints to spot customer concerns.

> Consumer insight means understanding what customers need, why they buy, what stops them, and how they behave. ICT tools gather and analyse evidence at low cost and high speed. **1. Surveys and forms.** Google Forms or a survey app can test price sensitivity, feature priorities, and awareness. Segment results by profession, age, location, and ride frequency rather than accepting one average answer. **2. Interviews and feedback.** Recorded interviews, support chats, and in-app feedback reveal the reason behind an answer. A team can tag recurring complaints without exposing personal data. **3. Web and app analytics.** Tools such as Google Analytics, Firebase Analytics, or Mixpanel show where users drop out, which features they repeat, and how acquisition channels convert. **4. Product tests and prototypes.** Figma clickable prototypes, usability sessions, A/B tests, and concierge MVPs show real behaviour before a full build. **5. Market and review mining.** Google Trends, keyword tools, and app-store reviews reveal rising needs and gaps left by competitors. **6. CRM and social listening.** CRM connects purchases and support history; social tools track complaints and conversations. **Example:** A ride-sharing startup tests a safety feature. Surveys show women value verified drivers and SOS functions. Session recordings show users cannot find the emergency button. App analytics show non-users leave before payment. The team then simplifies the feature, tests fare transparency, and measures repeat rides. ICT supplies evidence; interviews and observed behaviour explain why it matters.
>
> The process should combine tools rather than rely on one dashboard. Begin with a decision: whether to build a safety feature, what price to test, or which customer segment to target. Select only the tool that can reduce that decision's uncertainty. Separate behavioural evidence from stated preference because users may praise a feature but fail to use it. Check sample quality, consent, privacy, and possible bias. Store each finding beside the assumption it tests, then repeat it after a product change. This creates a learning loop: collect evidence, interpret it with customers, change the offer, and measure behaviour again. In Nepal, language and connectivity differences matter, so include users outside the capital and test low-data mobile flows. The result is not more data. It is a faster, safer decision about the customer problem.

*(366 words)*

## 2.9 Case study: Pathao Nepal — market entry and adaptations

| Stage | Pathao's public history | Why it worked | Lesson for a new entrant |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Initial entry** | Began with food delivery, then entered ride-sharing during the mobile-app and smartphone growth period | addressed a daily urban need while the operating habit was still new | enter one dense market and one clear use case first |
| **Driver network** | Used driver-partner participation rather than an employee-only model | expanded supply quickly with flexible demand | build supply and trust together; do not launch with riders but no reliable drivers |
| **Reach and trial** | Used online promotion, word of mouth, and a 2017 "Give 100, Get 100" referral offer | reduced the trust barrier between strangers | reduce first-use friction with transparent fares, visible identities, and referral support |
| **Local payment reality** | Expanded towards digital payment while users still used cash widely | matched the market instead of demanding instant digital-only behaviour | support familiar payment methods during transition; measure wallet adoption rather than assuming it |
| **Safety and trust** | Added driver verification, ratings, in-app support, and ride tracking | trust is a core product feature in a new mobility market | design safety into the service, not as an optional feature |
| **Service expansion** | Later added delivery, courier, and payment-related services | reused the same rider, driver, merchant, and payment network | expand only when the first service has strong repeat use and a dependable network |

**Exam conclusion:** Pathao's advantage came from sequence and adaptation: solve a frequent urban problem, build both sides of the network, reduce first-use risk, support local payment behaviour, and expand only after the core service worked. A new entrant should copy the method, not merely the super-app idea.

## 2.10 Case sub-parts (D22a, D22b)

### D22(a) · "Explain the steps you will follow for idea generation." [5] 🎯 5-mark

> **Shape: 4–5 steps, one sentence each, with Pathao context. ~150 words.**

As promoting manager for a Pathao-like ride-sharing app:

1. **Trend scan** — identify rising trends: smartphone penetration, digital payments, urban congestion, gig economy.
2. **Problem observation** — observe commuter pain: unsafe microbuses, unreliable taxis, no price transparency, cash-only.
3. **Technique application** — run SCAMPER on "taxi service" (Substitute app for phone, Combine ride+payment, Adapt driver-partner model).
4. **MVP definition** — define the riskiest assumption (will riders trust an app?) and build a concierge MVP (WhatsApp booking).
5. **Validation loop** — measure repeat rate, NPS, driver earnings; iterate before scaling.

*(156 words)*

### D22(b) · "How do you analyze the market?" [5] 🎯 5-mark

**Market-size terms:** **TAM** is the total market, **SAM** is the part the venture can serve, and **SOM** is the smaller part it can realistically win first.

> **Shape: TAM/SAM/SOM + competitor map + consumer insight. ~150 words.**

Market analysis for a Kathmandu ride-sharing app:

1. **TAM/SAM/SOM** — TAM: all frequent urban ride trips. SAM: target riders reachable through the chosen geography and channels. SOM: repeat riders the venture can serve in the first pilot.
2. **Competitor map** — Direct: Pathao, Tootle, inDrive. Indirect: microbuses, taxis. Wedge to test: **safety-first mobility** with verified drivers, SOS, and ride tracking.
3. **Consumer insight** — Price-sensitive students versus safety-focused professionals. Trust signals: driver photo, SOS button, insurance, fare transparency. Payment: wallet plus cash during the cash-to-digital transition.
4. **Regulatory scan** — Company registration, transport rules, driver-worker agreements, data privacy, and VAT/TDS obligations.

*(168 words)*
