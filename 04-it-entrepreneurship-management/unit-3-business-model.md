## 3.1 Business Model Canvas (BMC) — the 9 blocks (A5)

> **The Business Model Canvas is a one-page framework that describes how a venture creates, delivers, and captures value — nine building blocks covering customers, offer, infrastructure, and financial viability.**

### The 9 blocks (memorise in this order)

| # | Block | Question it answers | Nepal ride-share example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Customer Segments** | Who are we creating value for? | Riders (commuters, students, tourists), Drivers (partners), Advertisers |
| 2 | **Value Propositions** | What problem do we solve for each segment? | Riders: safe, affordable, transparent rides. Drivers: flexible income, low commission. |
| 3 | **Channels** | How do we reach and deliver to customers? | Mobile app (iOS/Android), website, referral programme, corporate partnerships |
| 4 | **Customer Relationships** | What relationship do we establish? | Automated (app), self-service, 24/7 support, loyalty programme, safety features |
| 5 | **Revenue Streams** | How do we make money? | Commission per ride (rate set by the venture), surge pricing, subscription (rider pass), ads |
| 6 | **Key Resources** | What assets do we need? | Platform (app + backend), driver network, brand, data, capital |
| 7 | **Key Activities** | What do we do every day? | Platform development, driver onboarding, safety ops, marketing, support |
| 8 | **Key Partnerships** | Who helps us? | Telecom (connectivity), payment gateways (eSewa/Khalti), insurance, regulators |
| 9 | **Cost Structure** | What are our major costs? | Platform dev, marketing, driver incentives, support, ops, legal/compliance |

### A5 · "What is Business Model Canvas?" [2] 🎯 2-mark

> The Business Model Canvas is a one-page framework describing how a venture creates, delivers, and captures value through nine blocks:
>
> (1) **Customer Segments** — who we serve (riders, drivers, advertisers).
> (2) **Value Propositions** — problems solved (safe, affordable rides; flexible driver income).
> (3) **Channels** — how we reach users (app, web, referrals).
> (4) **Customer Relationships** — automated app, 24/7 support, loyalty.
> (5) **Revenue Streams** — commission per ride (rate set by the venture), surge, subscriptions, ads.
> (6) **Key Resources** — platform, driver network, brand, data.
> (7) **Key Activities** — dev, onboarding, safety, marketing.
> (8) **Key Partnerships** — telecom, payments, insurance.
> (9) **Cost Structure** — dev, marketing, incentives, ops.

*(56 words. Definition + nine named blocks with one-line each.)*

### Board A3 · "Define business model." [2] 🎯 2-mark

> A business model explains how a venture creates, delivers, and captures value. It connects the target customer and its problem to the product, channels, relationships, activities, resources, partners, revenue, and costs. A strong model earns money because customer value exceeds delivery cost. For an IT startup, it also shows how the product scales and covers technology, people, marketing, and compliance.

*(60 words)*

### Board B3 · "Explain any five building blocks of the Business Model Canvas framework." [5] 🎯 5-mark

> The Business Model Canvas describes a venture through nine connected blocks. **1. Customer Segments** identifies who receives value, such as commuters, tourists, and drivers. **2. Value Propositions** states the problem solved and why the offer is better: safer, faster, or cheaper. **3. Channels** explains how customers are reached and served through an app, website, referral, or partnership. **4. Customer Relationships** covers acquisition, support, trust, loyalty, and feedback. **5. Revenue Streams** shows how money arrives through commission, subscription, advertising, or transaction fees. Other blocks cover key resources, activities, partnerships, and costs. The canvas is useful because it exposes weak assumptions. If customers are attractive but the revenue stream is unclear, the model is not ready for investment.
>
> The blocks should be drawn in the standard order and tested against evidence rather than treated as nine independent lists. For example, a strong value proposition is weak if the chosen channel cannot reach the segment affordably. Key resources and partnerships expose dependencies, while cost structure tests whether the revenue stream can support the promise. A canvas is finished only when each block connects to the others and the team can state the assumption that needs testing next.

*(194 words)*

## 3.2 Revenue sources and cost structure

### Identifying revenue sources (syllabus bullet)

| Type | Description | Ride-share example |
|---|---|---|
| **Transaction revenue** | per-use fee | commission per ride |
| **Recurring revenue** | subscription | rider monthly pass, driver premium |
| **Advertising revenue** | third-party ads | in-app banners, partnered offers |
| **Data/insights revenue** | aggregated, anonymised | traffic patterns to city planners |
| **Partnership revenue** | referral, affiliate | insurance upsell, vehicle financing |

### Cost structure analysis

| Cost type | Description | Ride-share example |
|---|---|---|
| **Fixed costs** | same regardless of volume | platform dev, office, core team salaries |
| **Variable costs** | scale with volume | driver incentives, payment gateway fees, support |
| **Customer acquisition cost (CAC)** | total sales and marketing cost to win one new customer | referral bonuses, ads, promotions |
| **Customer lifetime value (LTV)** | total revenue expected from one customer over time | average rides × commission × retention months |

**Unit economics** means whether one sale earns more than it costs to win and serve the customer. **Common rule of thumb:** LTV should be at least 3× CAC.

### Board C3 · "Describe revenue-stream types and explain the tools and importance of cost-structure analysis." [10] 🎯 10-mark

> A **revenue stream** describes how a business earns money. Common types include **transaction revenue**, charged per sale, ride, or order; **subscription revenue**, paid at a fixed interval; **usage-based revenue**, linked to consumption; **advertising and sponsorship revenue**; **licensing or subscription software revenue**; **transaction or marketplace fees** from other users; and **data or insight revenue** where lawful and anonymised. A venture can combine several streams, but each needs a target customer, price, billing method, and margin test. **Cost-structure analysis** identifies fixed costs, such as core salaries and software, and variable costs, such as payment fees, cloud usage, incentives, and commissions. Tools include accounting software, bank feeds, inventory systems, payroll platforms, spreadsheets, and unit-economics dashboards. The analysis matters because revenue alone hides whether the business is sustainable. It reveals gross margin, customer acquisition cost, contribution per transaction, break-even volume, and cash needs. Pricing, product scope, supplier contracts, and funding decisions should then follow the evidence. A high-revenue model with poor margins can consume cash quickly; a lower-revenue model may still survive if costs scale carefully.
>
> The analysis should connect every stream with the cost required to earn it. For one ride, subtract gateway charges, driver incentives, support time, payment collection, and mapped operating cost from the fare. For software, calculate hosting, support, development, and sales cost against recurring subscription revenue. For advertising, estimate the audience, sale rate, and revenue required to cover the campaign. This reveals whether a popular service creates value or merely creates work. A good dashboard can display revenue, direct cost, contribution margin, customer acquisition cost, payback period, and break-even volume by product and channel. It should also show trends and scenarios because one month's totals can hide seasonality or growing support load.
>
> The result guides action. A weak margin may require a higher price, fewer discounts, a lower-cost delivery channel, or removal of an unprofitable product. A high acquisition cost may require better onboarding or stronger referrals. Fixed costs that no longer match the stage should be reduced carefully. Managers should review the analysis monthly and explain every major change. The core principle is simple: revenue is an input, but contribution and cash are the outcome. Cost-structure analysis shows whether each revenue stream deserves to stay and what the venture must change before it scales.

*(376 words)*

## 3.4 Essential elements of a business plan (C21b)

> **A business plan is the detailed document that expands the BMC into an execution roadmap — used for internal alignment and external fundraising.**

### The 10 essential elements (memorise this list for C21b)

| # | Element | What it contains |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Executive Summary** | one-page snapshot: problem, solution, market, team, ask |
| 2 | **Company Overview** | mission, vision, legal structure, location, history |
| 3 | **Problem & Solution** | the pain point, your product/service, why now |
| 4 | **Market Analysis** | TAM/SAM/SOM, trends, target segments, competitive landscape |
| 5 | **Business Model** | BMC expanded: revenue streams, pricing, unit economics |
| 6 | **Marketing & Sales Strategy** | positioning, channels, funnel, CAC, retention, partnerships |
| 7 | **Operational Plan** | team, hiring, processes, tech stack, suppliers, milestones |
| 8 | **Financial Projections** | 3-year P&L, cash flow, balance sheet, assumptions, break-even |
| 9 | **Risk Management** | key risks (market, tech, regulatory, team), mitigations |
| 10 | **Funding Request / Appendix** | amount, use of funds, equity offered, cap table (who owns what), supporting documents |

### C21(b) · "What are the essential elements of a business plan?" [5] 🎯 5-mark

> The ten essential elements are:
>
> (1) **Executive Summary** — one page covering the problem, solution, market, traction, team, and funding ask.
> (2) **Company Overview** — mission, vision, legal structure, location, and stage.
> (3) **Problem and Solution** — the user's pain, the proposed product, and why it works now.
> (4) **Market Analysis** — TAM, SAM, SOM, trends, target segments, and competitors.
> (5) **Business Model** — the BMC, pricing, revenue streams, costs, and unit economics.
> (6) **Marketing and Sales Strategy** — positioning, target customer, channels, funnel, budget, retention, and sales motion.
> (7) **Operational Plan** — the team, hiring, processes, technology stack, suppliers, and milestones.
> (8) **Financial Projections** — three-year P&L, cash flow, balance sheet, assumptions, and break-even.
> (9) **Risk Management** — major market, technical, regulatory, financial, and team risks with mitigation.
> (10) **Funding Request and Appendix** — amount, use of funds, equity or debt terms, cap table, and supporting documents.
>
> Close with a clear implementation schedule so the reader can judge feasibility, not only ambition. These ten sections cover strategy, execution, money, and risk.

*(194 words. Ten named elements with one line of content each, plus a closing feasibility judgement.)*

### Board A5 · "Define business plan." [2] 🎯 2-mark

> A business plan is a roadmap explaining a venture's problem, solution, market, model, operations, marketing, financial needs, risks, and funding request. It helps founders test assumptions and align the team. Investors use it to judge whether the idea has a credible market, executable plan, goals, and clear use of funds. A plan states assumptions and updates them as evidence changes.

*(60 words)*

## 3.5 Financial projections (what to include)

| Projection | Horizon | Key assumptions to state |
|---|---|---|
| **Revenue model** | monthly, Year 1–3 | rides/day, avg fare, commission %, take rate |
| **Cost model** | monthly, Year 1–3 | fixed (dev, salaries), variable (incentives, gateway fees), CAC |
| **P&L (profit-and-loss statement)** | monthly Y1, quarterly Y2–3 | gross margin, EBITDA (earnings before interest, tax, depreciation, and amortisation), net profit |
| **Cash flow** | monthly Y1, quarterly Y2–3 | operating, investing, and financing cash; runway (months of cash left) |
| **Balance sheet** | Year 1–3 | assets, liabilities, and equity |
| **Break-even** | month | the point where total revenue equals total costs |
| **Unit economics** | per ride | LTV, CAC, payback period, contribution margin (revenue minus direct costs) |

## 3.8 Risk management strategies

| Risk category | Examples | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| **Market** | low adoption, competitor price war | differentiated wedge, referral loop, brand |
| **Technical** | app downtime, data breach | servers in multiple locations, encryption, security testing, incident-response plan |
| **Regulatory** | permit denial, law change, labour dispute | legal counsel, compliance calendar, driver contracts |
| **Financial** | cash runway, fraud, payment disputes | monthly forecasting, fraud detection, escrow |
| **Team** | co-founder conflict, key hire loss | vesting, clear roles, culture, retention bonuses |

### Board A7 · "What is risk management?" [2] 🎯 2-mark

> Risk management identifies possible threats, judges their likelihood and impact, and takes proportionate action before losses occur. Startups face market, technical, financial, legal, operational, and team risks. The process uses prevention, reduction, transfer, or acceptance, supported by owners, deadlines, budgets, and contingency plans. It also monitors warning signs and learns after incidents. The aim is to prevent avoidable surprises.

*(59 words)*

## 3.9 Case study: business models of emerging Nepali tech startups

> **Source limit:** This comparison uses publicly visible business models. It does not claim audited revenue, valuation, profit, or user figures for these companies.

| Venture | Customer segment and problem | Value proposition and channels | High-level revenue model | Key resources, partners, and main risk | Business-plan lesson |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **SastoDeal** | buyers and sellers who want a convenient online marketplace | product discovery, seller listing, delivery, and trust features through its website and app | marketplace commission and service charges around transactions; other service revenue depends on the current offer | seller and logistics network, payments, brand, and retail partnerships; main risks are trust, delivery economics, and low repeat purchase | define the commission logic, seller value, and cost of delivery before forecasting volume |
| **Khalti** | consumers and merchants who need simple digital payment | fast mobile payment, merchant QR acceptance, and digital services through the app and merchant network | transaction or merchant-service fees; revenue grows only when usage and merchant acceptance grow | payment licences, bank and telecom links, fraud controls, and merchant distribution; main risks are regulation, fraud, and trust | connect revenue to active use, repeat transactions, and merchant onboarding rather than total downloads |
| **Pathao** | riders, drivers, merchants, and delivery users in mobility and local services | on-demand transport and delivery, driver income opportunities, and later adjacent digital services through one app | transaction commissions and related service or subscription revenue; each service has a separate unit-economics question | driver and rider networks, payment and technology partners, brand, and operations; main risks are safety, worker classification, regulation, and cash burn | sequence expansion only after the core network works; state the unit economics and legal exposure for each new service |

**Exam comparison:** These startups sell different core promises — marketplace access, payment convenience, and mobility — but all rely on a two-sided network, trust mechanisms, strong operating processes, and careful unit economics. A business plan must state which customer pays, why the customer pays, what each side contributes, which partner supplies a critical capability, and which assumption must be tested first.

## 3.10 Case sub-part (D22c) — "How do you ensure the benefit and trust to rider?" [5]

> **Shape: BMC value proposition + trust mechanisms (5 specific). ~150 words.**

Ensuring rider benefit and trust in a Pathao-like ride-sharing app:

1. **Value proposition** — safe, affordable, transparent rides (BMC Block 2): upfront fare, no surge surprise, ETA accuracy.
2. **Verified drivers** — background check, licence verification, vehicle inspection, photo ID in app (BMC Block 7: safety ops).
3. **Insurance & SOS** — trip insurance (partner with insurer), in-app SOS button sharing live location with contacts + police (BMC Block 4: safety relationship).
4. **Rating & accountability** — two-way rating, driver deactivation for <4.0, rider refund for safety incidents (BMC Block 4: trust relationship).
5. **Fare transparency & support** — upfront pricing, receipt with route map, 24/7 in-app chat support, 30-min dispute resolution (BMC Block 4: support relationship).

*(156 words)*
